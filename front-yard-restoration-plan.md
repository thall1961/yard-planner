# Front Yard Restoration Plan

**Location:** Burleson, TX (North Texas — Blackland Prairie / Cross Timbers transition)
**Written:** Late July 2026 (highs in the upper 90s–100s)

## Diagnosis

- **Grass:** Common **bermudagrass** — thin and patchy, but alive. The backyard proves this yard can grow a good bermuda lawn.
- **Weeds filling the bare spots:**
  - Crabgrass (wider-bladed clumps)
  - Spotted spurge and oxalis (small-leaved mat weeds)
  - Bitterweed (*Helenium amarum*) — yellow button flowers, threadlike foliage; indicator of poor, dry, compacted soil
- **Pests:** Fire ant mounds (granular reddish-brown mounds)
- **Soil:** Heavy alkaline expansive clay, badly compacted, cracking in the classic honeycomb pattern from drought. Thin layer of tan, gravelly builder's fill in spots. Not dead — just compacted, bone dry, and low in organic matter. Bermuda thrives on Texas clay once it gets water and nitrogen.

## Key rule for right now

It's peak summer heat, so this is a **two-phase job**: keep everything alive and start recovery now; do the real weed reset in fall and next spring.

- **Do NOT** seed or lay sod in this heat.
- **Do NOT** blanket-spray herbicide on drought-stressed bermuda — you'll kill more grass than weeds.

## Phase 1 — Now through September (recovery)

1. **Water deeply and infrequently.** This is 80% of the fix. Cracked clay sheds water, so use **cycle-and-soak**: run each zone ~15 min, wait an hour, repeat (ideally a third cycle). Target **1–1.5" per week split into 2 deep waterings**. Check Burleson's summer watering restrictions (most Metroplex cities allow 2 days/week by address). After 2–3 weeks the cracks will close and water will soak in normally. **Full beginner walkthrough in the "Watering, step by step" section below.**
2. **Kill the fire ants first.** Broadcast a bait (Amdro or similar) over the whole yard, not just the mounds. Do this before working the soil.
3. **Fertilize lightly — but only once the grass is being watered consistently.** Never fertilize dry, unwatered bermuda in this heat. Two weeks after consistent watering starts, apply slow-release nitrogen (21-0-0 or a 3-1-2 ratio turf fertilizer) at **~½ lb N per 1,000 sq ft**. Repeat ~6 weeks later in early September. Bermuda + water + nitrogen = runners closing bare spots up to an inch a day.
4. **Mow weekly at 1.5–2"** once it's growing. Frequent mowing makes bermuda spread sideways instead of up. Leave the clippings.
5. **Spot-treat weeds only.** Hand-pull bitterweed and spurge (they come up easily from moist soil after watering), or spot-spray broadleaf weeds with a bermuda-safe product early in the morning on a sub-90° day. **Ignore the crabgrass this year** — it dies at first frost, and next year's crop is blocked by the pre-emergent below.

## Watering, step by step (beginner's guide)

### How many days a week?

**Two days a week. That's it.** More days is not better — it's actually worse.

- Pick the two days Burleson allows for your address (check the city's watering schedule page — most Metroplex cities assign days by even/odd house number). If there are no restrictions right now, just pick two days spaced apart, like **Monday and Thursday** or **Tuesday and Friday**.
- **Water in the early morning, ideally 4–8 AM.** Set the sprinkler or irrigation timer so it finishes around sunrise. Evening watering leaves grass wet overnight (invites fungus), and midday watering loses a lot to evaporation.
- Daily light sprinkling is the #1 beginner mistake. It keeps only the top inch of soil damp, so roots stay shallow and the lawn stays fragile. Two deep soakings force roots to chase water downward — that's what makes bermuda tough.

### How much water each time?

The goal is **½ to ¾ inch of water per watering day** (adding up to 1–1.5" per week). Since clay can only absorb it slowly, split each watering day into cycles:

- **Cycle 1:** run each zone/sprinkler spot ~15 minutes
- **Wait about an hour** (water soaks in, cracks stop swallowing it)
- **Cycle 2:** another ~15 minutes
- **Cycle 3 (if you can):** another 10–15 minutes

If you're using a hose-end sprinkler instead of an irrigation system, just move it around the yard in the same pattern: 15 minutes per spot, come back an hour later, repeat.

### The tuna can test (do this once, the first week)

Sprinklers vary wildly, so measure yours once instead of guessing:

1. Set out 4–6 empty tuna cans (or any straight-sided cans/cups) scattered across the yard.
2. Run your normal full watering (all cycles).
3. Measure the water depth in the cans with a ruler.

If you got about ½–¾", your run times are right. If you got ¼", roughly double your minutes. If one can is full and another is nearly empty, that dry zone explains a thin patch — adjust sprinkler placement.

### The screwdriver test (your weekly check)

The day after watering, push a long screwdriver into the soil:

- **Slides in 4–6" with moderate pressure** → water is getting down where roots need it. 
- **Stops hard at 1–2"** → water is running off before soaking in. Add a cycle or more wait time between cycles, not more days.

This one test replaces all guesswork. Do it weekly for the first month.

### When to adjust

- **100°+ stretch with no rain:** okay to add a third watering day *if allowed*, or just lengthen your two days a bit.
- **It rained ½" or more:** skip the next watering day. A cheap rain gauge ($5) takes the guessing out.
- **October and cooler:** drop back to about 1" per week, then in winter dormancy water only every 3–4 weeks if there's been no rain (yes, even brown dormant grass needs occasional water on this clay).

## How to know it's working (signs to watch for)

### Week 1–2
- **Water stops pooling/running off** and starts disappearing into the soil faster during cycles.
- **Soil cracks begin closing.** The honeycomb cracking visibly narrows as the clay rehydrates and swells.
- Weeds perk up first — annoying but actually a good sign: it means water is reaching the root zone. (This is when bitterweed and spurge pull out easily by hand.)

### Week 2–4
- **The existing grass turns a deeper green**, starting in the areas that were thin-but-alive.
- **Grass needs mowing again.** If you go from mowing "whenever" to genuinely needing it weekly, the lawn has woken up.
- The screwdriver slides in easily the day after watering.

### Week 4–8 (this is the exciting part)
- **Runners (stolons) appear** — look for wiry stems crawling horizontally across bare dirt at the edges of the patches, with little green leaves and roots at each joint. This is bermuda actively reclaiming ground, and it's THE sign the plan is working. Healthy watered/fertilized bermuda can extend runners ½–1" per day in August heat.
- Bare patches visibly shrink month over month. Take a phone photo from the same spot (e.g., the front porch) every Sunday — side-by-side photos will show progress your eyes miss day-to-day.
- Color evens out: less of the patchy green/tan mosaic, more uniform green.

### Warning signs (something needs adjusting)
- **Grass blades folded in half lengthwise, bluish-gray tint, or footprints that stay visible** after walking across it → drought stress. Water is not getting deep enough — recheck with the screwdriver and tuna cans.
- **Yellowing 1–2 weeks after fertilizing** despite good watering → possible fertilizer burn (too much) or iron issue from alkaline soil. Water it in heavily and wait; don't add more fertilizer.
- **Mushrooms, or circular brown patches that expand** → too much water or evening watering. Confirm you're watering mornings only, two days a week.
- **Nothing greening up at all after 3 weeks of consistent watering** → dig up a small chunk: white/tan roots and firm runners mean it's alive, just slow — keep going. Brown mushy roots mean that spot is dead and goes on the spring plug list.

## Phase 2 — Fall (September–October)

6. **Pre-emergent herbicide** (prodiamine, e.g. Barricade) around **mid-September** to block winter weeds.
7. **Core-aerate in early September** while bermuda is still growing — the single best fix for compacted clay. Water the day before, make two passes with a rented core aerator (or hire it out). Optionally top-dress with ¼" of compost right after; it works into the aeration holes and builds organic matter.
8. **Soil test** ($12–15, mail-in, Texas A&M AgriLife soil lab). North Texas clay is usually alkaline with plenty of phosphorus — the test tells you exactly what to skip and what to add.

## Phase 3 — Next spring (March–June)

9. **Pre-emergent again in early March** — this is what actually beats crabgrass.
10. Once nights stay above 65°: fertilize roughly monthly (½–1 lb N/1,000 sq ft) through summer, keep deep watering, keep mowing. Any bare patches left by May: plug with pieces of backyard bermuda or a few pieces of sod — it knits fast.

## Expectations and budget

- Noticeably greener in **3–4 weeks**
- Mostly covered by **October**
- Genuinely good-looking lawn by **next June**
- DIY budget: **~$150–250** (fertilizer, ant bait, pre-emergent, one aerator rental)
