💧 Watering Guide

Bermuda on North Texas clay — the whole plan in one page

Front Yard Backyard Putting Green Watering Guide
The one-sentence answer: water 2 days per week, in the early morning, deeply (multiple cycles), and do absolutely nothing on the other 5 days.

How many days a week?

Two days a week. That's it. Not three, not five, not "a little every day." More days is not better — it's actually worse.

  • Pick the two days Burleson allows for your address (most Metroplex cities assign days by even/odd house number). No restrictions? Just pick two days spaced apart, like Monday & Thursday or Tuesday & Friday.
  • Water in the early morning, ideally 4–8 AM. Evening watering leaves grass wet overnight (invites fungus); midday watering loses a lot to evaporation.
  • Daily light sprinkling is the #1 beginner mistake — it keeps only the top inch damp, so roots stay shallow and the lawn stays fragile.

Why only two days works in 100° heat: grass drinks from the soil around its roots, not the surface. One deep soak wets the clay 4–6 inches down, and clay holds that moisture for days — the grass sips from that reservoir until the next soak. Daily sprinkling never fills the reservoir; the sun burns off the top inch by noon.

"Nothing" really means nothing — no quick hose spritz because it looks hot out. Grass looking a bit dull on day 3 is normal; that's what trains roots to grow deeper.

What a watering day looks like

  1. ~4:30 AM — Cycle 1: each zone (or sprinkler position) runs ~15 minutes.
  2. Hour-long pause. The water soaks in instead of running down the curb — this pause is the whole trick with cracked clay.
  3. ~6:00 AM — Cycle 2: another ~15 minutes per zone.
  4. (If you can) ~7:15 AM — Cycle 3: another 10–15 minutes per zone.
  5. Done by ~8 AM. Don't touch the sprinkler until the next watering day.

Moving a hose-end sprinkler by hand? Doing the cycles from 6–9 AM is still fine — early-ish morning beats a perfect schedule you won't keep. Target is ½–¾" of water per watering day (1–1.5" per week total).

Measure once: the tuna can test

  1. Set out 4–6 straight-sided cans scattered across the yard.
  2. Run your normal full watering (all cycles).
  3. Measure the water depth with a ruler.

About ½–¾" → run times are right. Only ¼" → roughly double your minutes. One can full and another nearly empty → that dry zone explains a thin patch; adjust sprinkler placement.

Check weekly: the screwdriver test

The day after watering, push a long screwdriver into the soil:

  • Slides in 4–6" with moderate pressure → water is reaching the roots. ✅
  • Stops hard at 1–2" → water is running off before soaking in. Add a cycle or more wait time between cycles — not more days.

Common doubts, answered

"It's 103° out — surely it needs water today?"
No. The soil 4 inches down is still moist from the last deep watering (check with the screwdriver if you don't believe it). Heat stresses the blades; the roots are fine.
"The neighbors water every day and their lawn looks good."
It looks good despite daily watering, not because of it. Daily watering breeds fungus and shallow roots — you'll pass them by fall.
"The grass looks gray and dry the day after watering."
That's the real signal to act on: water isn't getting deep enough. Add a cycle or lengthen cycles on your two days, and re-run the tuna can test.
"I missed my watering day."
Water the next morning and slide back onto schedule. One late day changes nothing.
"It rained — do I still water?"
If it rained about ½" or more, that was a watering day. Skip the next scheduled one.
"Can I do one giant watering once a week instead?"
On this clay, no — it can't absorb a full week's water in one session even with cycles. Two sessions is the sweet spot.
